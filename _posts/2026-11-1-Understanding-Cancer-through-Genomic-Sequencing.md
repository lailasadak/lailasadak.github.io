Genomic sequencing is the process of finding an organism’s overall personalized set of genetic instruction by reading the order of its DNA. In DNA sequencing, we have 4 nitrogenous bases: adenine, thymine, guanine, and cytosine. Each of these bases stores genetic information and is apart of the composition of a nucleotide, which is a building block of DNA, along with deoxyribose and a phosphate group. So when we talk about genomic sequencing versus just DNA sequencing, we are talking about examining all of someone’s DNA, it is on a much larger scale rather than a specific gene. One type of genomic sequencing used in cancer research to combat this is NGS, Next Generation Sequencing. The way this genomic sequencing works is by that we can take bodily sample from someone such as saliva or blood and break down that sample into fragments of DNA. Then, researchers are able to put it into sequencing machinery where it is able to read its bases and determine its genetic code. By doing this, we are able to find out if any mutations have occurred by comparing it to a reference genome. These mutations can lead to tumors and some of these tumors can be identified as cancer. An important term to know when we talk about genomic sequencing are SNPs, Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms. It is basically what is considered a common type of genetic variation amongst the human population. This is not to get confused with a mutation because a SNP causes no harm whereas a mutation can sometimes cause harm which is how cancer is developed. The way researchers are able identify and differentiate the two this is because SNPs show up in at least 1% of the population whereas a mutation is rare. One way researchers can use these two in genomic sequencing is using SNPs to study how to minimize risks for diseases while mutations are used to find ways to control cancer. Two important types of genes are also needed to understanding cancer in relation to genomic sequencing: tumor suppressor genes and oncogenes. Driver genes are types of mutated genes that essentially allow cells to continuously grow and divide which then leads to cancer. Tumor suppressor genes are genes at help regulate cell growth. Oncogenes genes, on the other hand, is a type of driver gene starts off as a normal gene (protooncogenes) but when abnormally turn ed on, it can promote uncontrolled cell growth. If the tumor suppressor gene is mutated though, this will then cause uncontrolled cell growth because it has lost its function. These mutations in DNA ultimately affect the whole genome and affect the person affected by it. By changing the genes in the sequence, we lose the ability to control normal cellular division and therefore surrender to the accumulation of cells building up on one another. This build up of cells creates the tumor. Tumors can be harmless if they do not start to interact with important tissue and grow on vital organs. If this happens though, the tumor is basically like the new host of your body; you have no control over the old you. If it is not controlled, it can break off into your blood stream and continue to spread to other parts of your body. It quite literally sucks the nutrients out of your body. This is why research is so crucial. Research using genomic sequencing aids in the fight against cancer by learning how tumors evolve and devolve based on how someone’s DNA is sequenced in comparison to others. This in turn gives researchers and doctors the tools to figure out what treatments and care is best to get rid of the cancer and prevent it from coming back or spreading to the next generation.
