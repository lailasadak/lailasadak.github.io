We have all heard about cancer and how it has impacted our way of life, but what really is it? Cancer is abnormal separation of cells and it can reach many parts of the body such as the pancreas, kidneys, skin, etc. Today, we will be zooming in on cancer in the lung. Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer related deaths in the United States. The chance that someone gets lung cancer is 1 in 19 and lung cancer accounts for every 1 in 5 cancer deaths.  It is the 3rd most common cancer in the United States. Over a 5 year rate, the chance of survival is 28-30%. It is also the second most common cancer in both genders  and lung cancer is usually diagnosed in the senior age demographic. There are two ways that lung cancer is detected: with symptoms or no symptoms. You could ask for a screening even if you don’t have lung cancer symptoms because symptoms tend to show up as the cancer spreads more. If you have some base level symptoms like, a continuous cough, coughing up blood, chest pain/shortness of breath, and infections can all be a reason to go checked out. If you do figure out you have lung cancer, you will start to notice that are fatigued, losing weight, have no appetite, and have bone and nerve pain. Different testings for lung cancer depend on where you are at when you come in. These tests include, imaging, biopsies, and different sampling tests. The number one way to prevent lung cancer is to not smoke. Smoking is the leading cause to all cases of lung cancer in the United States due to how toxic tobacco is. Although one of the biggest factors into getting lung cancer is smoking, it can also be caused by prolonged radiation exposure, secondhand smoke, carcinogen exposure, and sometimes genetics can all play a role in someone’s chance of getting lung cancer. There are two types of cancer: small cell lung cancer and non-small cell lung cancer. Small lung cancer (SCLC) is less common than non-small lung cancer (NSCLC) with 13% being SCLC and the other 77% being NSCLC. It is because SCLC only really happens in people that have been smoking significantly over the course of many years. Within non small cancer on the other hand, we have different subtypes: squamous cell carcinoma, adenocarcinoma and large cell carcinoma. Not only are there different types, but there are different stages with each one getting more aggressive as the cancer progresses. In staging lung cancer, we have to look at 3 things: the tumor, the node, and the metastasis. We have to consider how big or small the tumor is, has the cancer made it to the lymph nodes, and if the primary tumor has invaded to other parts outside of the lung. Now that we have our 3 markers, we have to look at it within the two types of cancer: NSCLC and SCLC.  The SCLC as two stages: limited and extensive. We split this stage into only two due to what will be a better course of treatment for the patient. The limited stage means that the cancer is only located on one half of the lung and is best to be in one concentrated area. This stage is treated with only single radiation. With the extensive stage, the cancer has spread everywhere, that can be from the lungs all the way to even bone marrow. Compared to the SCLC, the NSCLC has 4 stages (numbered 0-4). Starting at ground 0, carcinoma in suti meaning the cancer is non invasive and is just surrounding the outer part of the airways. After stage 0, we go through the 4 stages but within those 4, they are all uniquely classified depending on many different factors. We use the letters T (for tumor), N (for node), and M (for metastasis). Accompanying those letters would be numbers and the higher the number, the more progressed or worse it is. Here I will only generalize each stage. Stage 1 is where the cancer is the smallest and only in the lung, not spread further to lymph nodes. Stage 2, the cancer has grew and branched out more, but still is in the general area of the lung. Stage 3, we see that the cancer has made it to the lymph nodes and tissue close by. In the final stage, the cancer has spread all around to the different parts of the body. Lung cancer comes near the end of the average human lifespan and this is because the the tissue inside the lungs don’t have pain receptors to communicate that a tumor is there as well as the fact that the lungs have a large capacity. When we refer to life expectancy we will use 5 years since the cancer was diagnosed to represent the survival rate. Early stages of lung cancer have a 55-65% survival rate, when it reaches the lymph nodes it becomes 25%, and advanced goes down to 8-10%. Extensive SCLC alone has a 6-12 month chance of survival. With all cancers. The best way to get rid of it will always be to remove the tumor; this requires surgery and it is best to remove it is small and still in its early stages. This is so the cancer doesn’t grow back. Each stage that we talked about has it own appropriate treatment. For limited SCLC, chemo radiation, immunotherapy, or PCI are all good options. For extensive SCLC, chemo immunotherapy, maintenance therapy, and palliative care. With NSCLC, we can try surgery, radiation therapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, immunotherapy, and palliative care. Keep in mind that each patient is different, each one has a different type and each one is in a different stage. Each one will have a formulated treatment and plan that caters to them to provide optimal health and recovery.
